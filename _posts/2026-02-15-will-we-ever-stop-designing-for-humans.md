I’m a grad student at UC Davis, living fairly close to San Francisco and the Bay Area. Lately, whenever I drive into the city, across the Bay Bridge, it feels like stepping into an apocalyptic dystopian fantasy. Everywhere I look, billboards scream: *“AI-ready!”,* *“Designed for AI!”*, *“Future-proof your business with AI!”* I think one even said, *“Why hire humans when you can get AI to do their job!?”*

The city hums with ambition, automation, and algorithms, as if every building, every street corner, and every interface is being optimized not for humans, but for some invisible machine that watches and judges your every move.

It makes me wonder. Are we still designing for humans, or are humans becoming secondary, a variable in a system built to satisfy AI expectations?

> Will AI eventually make ‘Human Centered Design’ obsolete?
{: .pullquote}

## The Black Box Problem

Let’s start with a simple truth that tends to get lost in the AI hype: most people, including many of the engineers who build them, cannot fully explain why a modern AI system does what it does. Deep neural networks distribute their “reasoning” across billions of mathematical connections, making their logic as difficult to trace as the neural activity in a human brain.

This matters enormously for design. When you’re building an interface for a human user, you can watch them struggle. You can ask them why they clicked the wrong button. You can run a usability test and observe confusion in real time. When you’re designing for an AI intermediary — a recommendation algorithm, a content moderation system, a search engine — you can’t have that conversation. The system doesn’t tell you why it made a choice. It just makes the most likely guess.

## The Chain Reaction

AI systems are popularly getting stacked on top of each other to perform different functions and make different decisions for a software system. This has paved the way to prioritize customization and personalization a lot more in the past couple of years than ever before. Every interface is tailor-made to fit you, and only you. Or so you believe.

Every layer in the agentic system feeds a probabilistic decision to the next. Between the system and the human, there are more layers. But at the end of the chain, you are in charge of the buttons you push. You still make the final decision. The choice that truly matters.

But given this choice, one must also remember that with every decision, an agent is erasing the outliers, erasing the mess, the nuances, the weirdness of what makes us unique. So it is important to be aware of the paradox in this era of hyper-personalization. As we move towards super-custom interfaces, we are also being pushed towards an intellectual genericness.

## The Illusion of Control

Every stacked AI system has a paper trail of human decisions behind it. Someone chose the training data. Someone defined the optimization function. Someone decided which features matter and which can be discarded. Someone determined what “good” performance looks like and what trade-offs are acceptable.

But here’s the critical insight from recent AI ethics research: **meaningful human control** is unity of human understanding, intervention, and the traceability of responsibility in AI decision-making. You can’t be held accountable for a system you don’t understand. You can’t intervene effectively in a process that’s invisible to you.

If no one seems responsible when something goes wrong, we should look at who made the decisions that built the system in the first place and who expected personal benefits when exercising that control. The people who designed the system, trained the models, and deployed the agents don’t get to claim “the algorithm decided” when things go wrong. Algorithmic responsibility laundering doesn’t hold up.

Responsibility assignments cannot be satisfied technologically. Responsibility requires a human in the loop, because we can’t hold machines responsible in any meaningful sense. Crucially, the location of that human matters. If our primary concern is assigning responsibility, we might insert a human in a different part of the algorithm’s lifecycle than if our primary concern is safety. A developer, a deployment team, a user — each occupies a different point in the chain, and each holds a different kind of accountability.

So when you ask who is in control, the answer is: **people are,** even when it doesn’t feel like it. Even when the system operates at a scale and speed that makes individual intervention impossible. The control was exercised earlier, in choices about architecture, data, optimization targets, and deployment contexts. Those choices shaped everything that followed. The danger isn’t that AI is autonomous. The danger is that **we pretend it is**, because pretending absolves us of responsibility for the values it enforces.

## Why Humans Cannot Be Removed from the Loop.

**At initiation,** AI systems don’t decide what problems are worth solving. A recommendation algorithm doesn’t wake up one morning and think, “I should help people discover new music.” A hiring system doesn’t decide that efficiency in screening resumes is more important than fairness in opportunity. These are **human choices,** choices about what to build, for whom, and why. A system in which humans and AI interact should have an explicitly defined domain of morally loaded situations within which the system ought to operate. Defining that domain is a distinctly human responsibility because it requires understanding context, power dynamics, and consequences that extend beyond any dataset.

AI is a tool. Tools don’t set their own purpose. Someone — a designer, a product manager, an executive, a policy-maker — sets the purpose. And that purpose embeds values, whether acknowledged or not.

**At the destination:** The output of an AI system doesn’t land in a vacuum. It lands in someone’s life. A recommendation shapes what they see, and therefore what they think is possible. A credit decision determines whether they can buy a home. A hiring algorithm decides whether they get an interview. Humans are believed, unlike AI, to be capable of judging the context and determining whether a considered action meets, or does not meet, ethical guidelines.

Context matters. A recommendation system might surface a song because it matches your listening history. But it doesn’t know if you’re playing it at a funeral, or a wedding, or the moment you learned you got the job, or the night you broke up. An AI model processes patterns. A human lives a life.

AI operates in the middle. It processes, filters, ranks, and recommends. But it doesn’t initiate meaning, and it doesn’t bear consequences. Humans do both. That’s not a limitation of current AI — it’s a fundamental asymmetry that no amount of optimization will overcome. You can’t automate moral responsibility. You can only try to hide it.

## Will we ever stop designing for Humans?

We build systems to make life easier, faster, and more predictable. But as those systems grow more capable, they also grow more influential, shaping the choices available to us and the paths we can take. The challenge isn’t that AI removes humans from the loop. The challenge is that it makes the loop harder to see. And in that invisibility, accountability dissolves. Agency becomes theater. Choice becomes compliance dressed up as personalization.

So we return to the same questions.

Are we still designing for humans, or are humans slowly becoming variables in systems designed to satisfy algorithmic expectations?

When every interface is shaped by layers of probabilistic decisions — each one filtering, ranking, optimizing for goals you didn’t set — what does it really mean to be the “user”? Are you the person the system serves, or the data it processes?

As our tools grow more capable of predicting and influencing behavior, will there come a point where designing for humans begins to feel indistinguishable from designing for the systems that interpret them?

Maybe the answer isn’t fully clear yet. But the urgency of the question — the fact that it feels more pressing with each new AI capability announced, with each interface optimized for engagement over understanding — might be the most important warning we have.

Because here is what we know for certain: the systems we build today will shape the choices available to people for years. They will determine what feels possible, what feels normal, and what feels inevitable.

The systems we build encode the future we believe is possible. So the real question, the one that will determine whether AI amplifies or erodes human flourishing, is:

> Whose future are we building? And who gets to decide?
{: .pullquote}
